### 2. OpenBB's provider abstraction is the pattern to copy verbatim — a four-method Fetcher ABC plus a permissive Pydantic Data model  
`confidence: high`

Read from source on 2026-08-24. `openbb_platform/core/openbb_core/provider/abstract/fetcher.py` defines `class Fetcher(Generic[Q, R])` with `require_credentials = True` (overridable) and exactly four static methods: `transform_query(params: dict) -> Q` (raise NotImplementedError), `extract_data(query, credentials)` / `aextract_data(query, credentials)` (async preferred — `__init_subclass__` raises NotImplementedError unless one is implemented, and aliases `extract_data = aextract_data` when both exist), and `transform_data(query, data, **kwargs) -> R | AnnotatedResult[R]`. Q is bound to `QueryParams`, D to `Data`. `abstract/data.py` defines `class Data(BaseModel)` with `model_config = ConfigDict(extra="allow", populate_by_name=True, ...)` and a `__alias_dict__: dict[str, str]` class attribute mapping canonical field names to per-provider aliases. **Why it matters.** You get a battle-tested provider-abstraction design for free without taking the AGPL: `extra="allow"` means a provider's extra fields survive rather than being silently dropped; `__alias_dict__` is where per-vendor field-name drift is absorbed; the query/extract/transform split is exactly the seam that contract tests and golden-file fixtures attach to. The disappearance of the polygon provider from the tree is itself evidence of the vendor-churn cadence you must engineer for.

### 9. SEC EDGAR imposes a 10 req/sec ceiling, a declared User-Agent, and — the non-obvious part — retroactively deletes and rewrites filings  
`confidence: high`

Verified 2026-08-24 from sec.gov/os/accessing-edgar-data. "Current max request rate: 10 requests/second." You must "declare your [bot/User-Agent] in request headers"; undeclared automated traffic is treated as a botnet and throttled — during this research SEC returned "Request Rate Threshold Exceeded" pages to plain curl. The critical operational clause: "Filings are sometimes authorized by SEC staff for removal or correction ... including, but not limited to, the document was submitted for the wrong filer, the document was a duplicate ... unreadable ... or the document contained sensitive information. Corrections processed during a given business day will be incorporated in the indexes built that evening. However, removals processed on subsequent business days will not be reflected in any previous daily, feed, or oldload index. The full and quarterly index files are rebuilt weekly, early on Saturday mornings, so that any post-acceptance correction (PAC) deletes or updates are incorporated." On the ingestion side, edgartools (PyPI v5.52.0, released 2026-08-23 — one day before this research, MIT licensed) is the healthiest option, alongside Arelle (arelle-release 2.44.4, 2026-08-18, Apache-2.0) for XBRL.

**Why it matters.** A naive design harvests daily index files once and treats its local Form 4 store as append-only. It is not: filings are deleted and amended after the fact, and the daily indexes are never retro-corrected — only the weekly Saturday rebuild carries PAC deletes. Without a scheduled weekly reconciliation against the rebuilt full/quarterly indexes, an insider-intelligence database silently accumulates phantom transactions and diverges permanently from EDGAR. For a product whose whole claim is "who is buying, who is dumping", that is a credibility-ending failure mode, and it is invisible until someone checks a specific name by hand. The 10 req/s ceiling also caps full-corpus backfill throughput and must be budgeted into the schedule.

### 10. Beneficial-ownership deadlines were compressed in 2024, and the real insider signal lives in fields most vendors do not expose  
`confidence: medium`

Verified from the SEC press release (2023-219) on 2026-08-24: initial Schedule 13D is now "5 business days" (reduced from 10 days), and 13D amendments are due "within two business days". Revised Schedule 13G deadlines took effect 30 September 2024; structured-data (XML) filing became mandatory for both 13D and 13G on 18 December 2024, with other amendments effective 90 days after Federal Register publication. Beyond that, and at medium confidence from domain knowledge rather than verified this session: Section 16 Form 4 remains due within two business days of the transaction; 13F remains 45 days after quarter end with a $100M threshold. The signal-quality point: the analytically decisive Form 4 fields are the transaction-code column and the 10b5-1 checkbox, not the share count. Code S (open-market sale) means something entirely different from code F (shares withheld for tax on vesting) or code G (gift), and a sale executed under a pre-adopted Rule 10b5-1 plan carries near-zero information versus a discretionary sale. The Dec-2022 10b5-1 amendments added mandatory cooling-off periods and the Form 4 checkbox precisely to make this distinguishable.

**Why it matters.** Three exploitable asymmetries. (1) The 2-business-day 13D amendment window makes activist accumulation observable close to real time — a live alerting surface that quarterly-13F products structurally cannot offer. (2) Mandatory XML for 13D/G since Dec 2024 means the historically painful part of ownership parsing is now machine-readable, collapsing what used to be a large NLP effort. (3) Most consumer insider screeners aggregate dollar value and ignore transaction codes and the 10b5-1 checkbox, which is why they produce famously noisy signals — normalising exactly those fields is the cheapest large differentiator available. Conversely, 13F is a lagging, long-only, US-listed, no-shorts dataset subject to confidential-treatment requests; presenting it as "who owns it" without those caveats is a correctness bug.

### 15. The AI knowledge layer is cheap to run and expensive to make trustworthy — concrete unit economics  
`confidence: high`

Anthropic model pricing (per the bundled claude-api reference, cached 2026-06-24, verified against it 2026-08-24): Claude Opus 5 (`claude-opus-5`) 1M context, $5.00/MTok input, $25.00/MTok output; Claude Sonnet 5 (`claude-sonnet-5`) 1M context, $3.00/$15.00 with a $2.00/$10.00 introductory rate running through 2026-08-31 (i.e. it expires in one week); Claude Haiku 4.5 (`claude-haiku-4-5`) 200K context, $1.00/$5.00; Claude Fable 5 1M context, $10.00/$50.00. The Message Batches API runs asynchronously at 50% cost. Prompt caching is prefix-match with a ~1,024-token minimum cacheable prefix and a maximum of 4 breakpoints per request; any byte change anywhere in the prefix invalidates everything after it, and render order is tools then system then messages. Worked estimate for a nightly knowledge-base authoring pass over ~500 names: ~50k input + ~5k output tokens per name on Opus 5 = $0.25 + $0.125 = ~$0.375/name, ~$187/night, ~$5,600/month at full daily refresh — falling to ~$2,800/month via the Batch API, and far lower again with a staleness-driven scheduler that only re-authors names with new filings (realistically 5-15% of the universe on any given day, i.e. ~$150-800/month).

**Why it matters.** The cost is not the constraint — a nightly incremental pipeline lands in the low hundreds of dollars per month, comfortably inside a single-user budget and roughly the price of one Koyfin Premium seat. The constraint is correctness. A knowledge base that asserts a wrong insider transaction or a wrong compensation threshold is worse than no knowledge base, because the user will act on it. This forces three non-negotiable design commitments: every generated claim carries a provenance pointer to a specific EDGAR accession number and text span; the KB is versioned and diffable so a bad generation can be rolled back and, crucially, so the user can see *what changed and why*; and an eval harness with a frozen gold set gates publication of each nightly build. Budget the eval harness as a first-class module (see effort estimates), not as testing overhead — it is the moat.

### 17. Symbology and identifiers are an under-costed licensing minefield, especially for the global equities promise  
`confidence: low`

Medium-to-low confidence on the licensing specifics; high confidence on the engineering burden. There is no free, complete, global security master. Bloomberg's OpenFIGI is openly licensed and free, and is the only identifier scheme in the set that carries no redistribution fee. CUSIP is administered commercially (CUSIP Global Services) and has historically asserted licensing rights over the *storage and redistribution of CUSIP databases*, even though individual CUSIPs appear in public 13F filings — 13F holdings tables are literally keyed on CUSIP, so an ownership module inherently accumulates a CUSIP-keyed dataset. ISIN is administered nationally through ANNA and is also commercially licensed in aggregate. Exchange tickers are neither unique nor stable across venues, and the same economic entity may carry different local tickers, currencies and lot conventions in each listing. Separately, corporate-action and adjustment handling differs by vendor: every provider back-adjusts splits and dividends on its own convention, so the same symbol and date will return different "close" values from EODHD, Massive and Databento.

**Why it matters.** Two distinct traps. Legally, an ownership/13F module that persists a CUSIP-keyed master is walking into a licensing conversation nobody budgets for; the mitigation is to key internally on FIGI, treat CUSIP as a transient join key from the filing rather than a stored dimension, and never expose a CUSIP-searchable database as a product feature. Engineering-wise, cross-vendor price disagreement is the single most common source of "the app is wrong" support tickets in this product class, and it is not a bug — it is convention drift. Golden-file fixtures must pin both adjusted and unadjusted series per provider (the canonical test case being a known split, e.g. AAPL's 4:1 on 2020-08-31), and the UI must surface which provider produced a given series rather than presenting a single anonymous truth.

## Recommended decisions

### Build on OpenBB (ODP) as a dependency, or build beside it copying its patterns?

**Recommendation.** Build BESIDE it. Do not take openbb as a distributed dependency. Reimplement the Fetcher pattern — transform_query / extract_data|aextract_data / transform_data — and a permissive Pydantic-style Data model with a per-provider alias dictionary, in your own codebase under your own licence. Optionally run openbb-api locally as a developer-only research scratchpad that never ships in the installer.

**Rationale.** The repo is AGPL-3.0-only with no commercial exception (verified in LICENSE, 2026-08-24). Distributing a Windows binary that links openbb obliges you to offer complete corresponding source of the combined work under AGPL — which forecloses every monetisation path in this brief. The abstraction itself is only a few hundred lines of design and is worth far more than the 33 bundled providers, most of which are free public sources you would integrate directly anyway. OpenBB's own pivot away from the desktop confirms the dependency would also be pointed away from your use case over time.

**Rejected.** Taking the AGPL dependency and open-sourcing the terminal (forecloses monetisation and gives away the incentives lens); negotiating a commercial OpenBB licence (no published path, and creates a strategic dependency on a company that just exited your niche); using OpenBB Workspace as the UI (cloud-hosted, not native, not multi-monitor, and Lite is a $2,400/yr team licence).

**Cost.** 6-8 engineer-weeks to build the provider abstraction plus the first three providers with contract tests. Versus an unbounded legal and strategic cost for the AGPL path.

### Charting engine: Lightweight Charts, TradingView Advanced Charts, a commercial .NET control, or build?

**Recommendation.** Prototype on Lightweight Charts in a WebView2 host to reach the walking skeleton in week 2, but treat it as explicitly disposable and budget a commercial .NET chart control (SciChart or LightningChart class) as the v1 answer. Make the decision at the end of Phase 1, not at the start, and keep the chart behind an interface from day one.

**Rationale.** Lightweight Charts is Apache-2.0 but legally requires the TradingView attribution notice and a visible link — the library ships an attributionLogo option precisely to satisfy this (verified in the repo README, 2026-08-24). Carrying a direct competitor's logo on every chart in a premium terminal is untenable long-term, and its Python wrapper is ~2 years stale. Advanced Charts is 'free' but gated behind an application and agreement whose terms are not public, creating both calendar risk and a dependency on a competitor's goodwill. A commercial control converts an unbounded 10-16 engineer-week render-loop problem into a bounded 3-5 week integration and is the single highest-leverage buy decision in the project.

**Rejected.** Building the render loop from scratch (this is the module that always overruns by 3x in this product class — 60fps crosshair-synced multi-pane rendering over 500k bars is a specialist problem); shipping with the TradingView logo permanently (cheap, but permanently undermines the positioning against a $59.95/mo competitor).

**Cost.** Commercial control: budget USD 1,000-3,000 per developer per year (SciChart pricing could not be verified — the pricing page returned 403 on 2026-08-24 — so treat this as an estimate to confirm before committing). Integration 3-5 engineer-weeks versus 10-16 to build.

### Which data providers for the v1 stack, and at what monthly cost?

**Recommendation.** Tier deliberately: EODHD All-In-One at $99.99/mo ($999.90/yr) as the broad global equities + fundamentals + FX baseline; Massive Stocks Developer at $79/mo for US depth and better intraday, upgrading to Stocks Advanced at $199/mo only if the user genuinely needs real-time rather than 15-minute delayed; SEC EDGAR direct via edgartools (free, MIT) for all filings and the entire incentives pillar; ORTEX Basic at $49/mo or $468/yr for short interest and borrow cost. Skip Databento entirely at v1. Target all-in ~$230-330/month for a single non-professional user.

**Rationale.** The incentives pillar — the actual differentiator — runs entirely on free EDGAR data, so premium market-data spend buys context, not differentiation. Fifteen-minute delayed data at $79/mo is sufficient for a research terminal used by a discretionary analyst-trader and is dramatically cheaper to license if the product ever ships to a second user. Databento's $199/mo Standard tier is aimed at tick-level and order-book work this read-only product does not need, and its real value here ($1,750/mo Plus with external distribution rights) is a monetisation-stage decision, not a v1 one.

**Rejected.** Single-vendor dependency (guarantees a total outage on the next Polygon-to-Massive-style event); free-tier-only using yfinance (Yahoo ToS is personal-use-only, which is unfixable and legally forecloses distribution); Databento at v1 (over-specified for read-only research, and its L2/L3 depth is wasted).

**Cost.** ~$230/month single-user with delayed data ($99.99 + $79 + $49), rising to ~$350/month with real-time US equities. Roughly one Koyfin Premium seat plus one TradingView Ultimate seat.

### UI framework for a native, GPU-fast, multi-monitor Windows terminal

**Recommendation.** WPF on .NET 8/9 as the primary recommendation, with Avalonia as the considered alternative if cross-platform optionality ever matters. Reject Electron outright.

**Rationale.** WPF has the deepest ecosystem of mature commercial financial charting and data-grid controls — the exact components this product must buy rather than build — plus proven per-monitor DPI v2 support and the largest body of prior art for docking/window-management in trading applications. Avalonia's core is MIT and free (Plus €299/yr/seat, Pro €899/yr/seat, Enterprise €6,999/yr/seat, verified 2026-08-24), and it is the right call if Linux or macOS ever enter scope, but it has a thinner commercial financial-control ecosystem today. Electron is rejected because per-window memory overhead makes a genuine multi-monitor, many-window terminal layout impractical, and because it forfeits the single most defensible reason for this product to exist as a desktop application.

**Rejected.** WinUI 3 (still the weakest third-party control ecosystem for financial visualisation and the most churn-prone API surface); Electron or Tauri (a web app in a frame — concedes the native advantage that is the strongest leg of the gap thesis); Qt (excellent technically, but commercial licensing is costly for a solo developer and the C++ path slows iteration substantially).

**Cost.** WPF: framework free; commercial chart and grid controls USD 1,000-3,000/dev/yr. Avalonia: €0 core, €299-899/yr/seat for tooling and premium controls.

### Code signing approach and when to start it

**Recommendation.** Use Azure Artifact Signing (renamed from Azure Trusted Signing; docs updated 2026-08-11) with the Basic SKU, and START THE IDENTITY VALIDATION IN WEEK 1 of the project, before writing significant code. If the developer is not resident in the US or Canada, incorporating an eligible legal entity is a prerequisite and must begin immediately.

**Rationale.** Verified constraints make this a critical-path scheduling item rather than a release-time chore: identity validation takes 1 to 20 business days and can take longer if additional documentation is requested; only three documentation upload attempts are permitted; any post-issuance correction requires an entirely new identity validation that disrupts certificates already in use; and — decisively — 'Individual developers must be located in the United States or Canada', while organisational Public Trust is limited to US, Canada, EU, UK, Australia, New Zealand, Japan, South Korea, Singapore, Switzerland, Norway and Israel. Artifact Signing's cloud model also works cleanly in CI without a hardware token, which a traditional EV certificate does not. Verified quotas are Basic 5,000 signatures/month and Premium 100,000/month.

**Rejected.** Traditional OV certificate (no hardware token but poor SmartScreen reputation and painful CI integration); traditional EV certificate on a hardware token (instant SmartScreen reputation, but the token makes CI signing genuinely awkward and costs USD 300-600/yr); shipping unsigned (SmartScreen will actively block, which is fatal for a paid professional tool).

**Cost.** Base monthly prices are NO LONGER PUBLICLY LISTED as of 2026-08-24 — the pricing table renders every money cell as '$-' and directs to a sales quote. Do not put a number in the budget; obtain a quote. Historically-cited figures around $10/mo Basic are unverifiable now and should not be relied on.

### Is the four-pillar gap thesis correct as stated?

**Recommendation.** Adopt three of the four pillars and demote the fourth. Refined positioning: 'The incentive-first research terminal — a native Windows workstation that treats who benefits as the primary index into a company, with a Claude-authored, citation-bound, versioned knowledge base, and macro/FX as context rather than a second product.'

**Rationale.** Pillar (a), native multi-monitor research desktop, is the strongest leg and is verified vacant: OpenBB abandoned the desktop terminal for a cloud Workspace, and every prosumer research competitor (Koyfin, TIKR, Finviz, BamSEC, ORTEX, Unusual Whales, AlphaSense, Fiscal.ai) is browser-only, while the surviving native platforms (Sierra Chart, NinjaTrader, MultiCharts, AmiBroker) are execution and TA tools, not research terminals. Pillar (b), incentives as a first-class lens, is genuinely unowned — six vendors each hold a fragment and the global insider layer (2iQ, Smart Insider) is enterprise-priced with no public rate card — though the moat is normalisation quality, not the idea. Pillar (c), a curated versioned AI knowledge layer, is the most defensible and is clearly differentiated from the generic RAG chatbots now shipping everywhere (OpenBB Copilot at 20 queries/day, Mr. Whale, WarrenAI). Pillar (d), FX and equities as symmetric surfaces, is the weak leg: TradingView Premium already delivers excellent FX charting at $59.95/mo, FX data has no consolidated tape or authoritative volume, and — decisively — a currency pair has no issuer, no insider, no Form 4, no 13F and no proxy statement, so the differentiating pillar simply does not apply to it.

**Rejected.** Keeping FX as a co-equal asset class (spends 5-8 engineer-weeks building a second full market surface with incompatible semantics to buy a feature a $59.95/mo incumbent already does better, while generating zero leverage on pillar 2); dropping FX entirely (loses genuinely useful cross-asset and macro-positioning context that reinforces the 'who is positioned and why' narrative).

**Cost.** Demoting FX to macro context saves roughly 3-5 engineer-weeks in v1 and removes an entire class of ongoing market-semantics maintenance.

### Phased roadmap and realistic solo-developer effort

**Recommendation.** Four phases, ~80-125 engineer-weeks total to v1 (18-28 months solo at a sustainable pace). Phase 0, Walking Skeleton, weeks 1-6 (4-6 ew): app shell, docking, one chart, one provider behind the abstraction, local DuckDB store — plus start code-signing identity validation on day one. Phase 1, MVP, weeks 7-26 (~30-40 ew cumulative): equities charting, watchlists, EDGAR ingestion, Form 4 Signal Decoder, Cluster Buy Detector, first Incentive Map, and a hand-curated knowledge base with Provenance-Bound Ask. Phase 2, v1, weeks 27-52: Ownership Delta Tape, 13D/G Activist Watch, Filing Diff Viewer, Insider-Aware Alerts, multi-monitor workspaces, signed installer with auto-update and alpha/beta/stable channels, Provider Health Console. Phase 3, v2, weeks 53-90: Paid To Make The Number compensation module, scheduled Claude KB authoring with the Eval Gate, Signal Backtest Harness, macro positioning context, non-US insider coverage. Per-module estimates: charting 10-16 ew built or 3-5 bought; provider abstraction plus three providers plus contract tests 6-8; time-series store and corporate actions 4-6; EDGAR ingestion 8-12; insider intelligence lens 6-10; ownership/13F engine 4-6; compensation module 6-10; AI knowledge layer with eval harness 8-14; multi-monitor layout persistence 3-5; installer/update/signing/CI/crash 4-6; screener/watchlists/alerts 4-6; polish, performance and QA 6-10.

**Rationale.** The schedule reliably slips in five specific places in this product class, and each is visible in the estimates above: the charting engine (always ~3x the naive estimate, which is precisely why it should be bought); data normalisation and corporate actions (invisible in mockups, endless in practice, and the top source of 'the app is wrong' reports); window and layout persistence across monitor-topology changes (looks trivial, is not); installer, updater and signing (dominated by external validation lead times measured in business days, not engineering); and the last 10% of the AI layer's factual reliability (which is why the Eval Gate is scoped as a P0 module rather than as test overhead). Front-loading EDGAR and the incentives lens into Phase 1 is deliberate: it is the differentiator, it runs on free data, and it de-risks the thesis before any significant market-data spend is committed.

**Rejected.** Building charting and market data first and adding incentives later (defers the only differentiating work behind the most commoditised and most overrun work, and burns data spend before the thesis is validated); attempting simultaneous equities and FX in the MVP (doubles market-semantics surface area for no differentiation).

**Cost.** 80-125 engineer-weeks to v1. Running costs at v1, single user: ~$230-330/month data, ~$40-80/month CI, $26/month Sentry Team, ~$150-800/month Claude for incremental KB authoring, plus $1,000-3,000/year chart controls.

### How to survive data-provider breakage as a structural certainty rather than an incident

**Recommendation.** Adopt a five-layer defence and build it into Phase 0, not retrofitted: (1) every provider behind the Fetcher-pattern abstraction with base URLs in configuration, never hardcoded; (2) nightly contract tests firing real requests against every live provider on a small fixed budget; (3) golden-file fixtures pinned per provider per schema version, covering both adjusted and unadjusted series across a known corporate action such as the AAPL 4:1 split on 2020-08-31; (4) a schema-drift detector that hashes response shape and alerts on any change before it reaches the UI; (5) synthetic canaries asserting known values for known symbol/date pairs. Surface all of it in the Provider Health Console.

**Rationale.** Provider churn is not a tail risk, it is the base rate, and this research session caught two instances inside a single planning window: polygon.io issued a 301 to massive.com (breaking hostnames, docs links, API-key domains and allow-lists simultaneously), and the polygon provider extension disappeared from OpenBB's provider tree entirely. Microsoft renamed Trusted Signing to Artifact Signing in the same period, changing the CLI surface. Golden files alone are insufficient because they pass while live APIs break; live contract tests alone are insufficient because they are noisy and cost money. The combination catches both silent schema drift and hard outages, and the health console converts what would otherwise be a mysterious wrong number into a diagnosable event — which is the single highest-frequency support cost in this product class.

**Rejected.** Mocking everything in tests (passes green while production is broken — the classic and most common failure); no abstraction with direct SDK calls (a single rebrand becomes a multi-week refactor); relying on vendor status pages and changelogs (schema drift is routinely undocumented and typically ships without announcement).

**Cost.** ~2 engineer-weeks incremental on top of the provider abstraction, plus a few dollars per month in contract-test API consumption. The cheapest insurance in the entire plan.

### Monetisation path and where the licensing cliffs sit

**Recommendation.** Stay explicitly single-user through v1 and design the architecture so the differentiating value survives on delayed and end-of-day data. If commercialisation is ever contemplated, the sequenced path is: sell the analysis layer over EDGAR data (which is public domain and carries no redistribution fee) first; add delayed market data second; and treat real-time redistribution as a separate business decision requiring exchange vendor agreements, not as a feature toggle.

**Rationale.** The cliff is verified, sharp and non-linear. At one self-directed non-professional user, all-in data costs ~$230-330/month. At user number two you are a vendor and redistributor: Massive/Polygon individual plans are contractually marked 'Non-pros only'; Databento prices external distribution rights at $1,750/month on an annual contract, 8.8x its $199 single-user Standard tier; and exchange vendor agreements bring subscriber reporting, audit rights and per-subscriber pass-through fees. A second, independent cliff sits on the user side: if the trader registers as an investment adviser, manages external capital, or is compensated for analysis, they are reclassified Professional and per-exchange fees rise by roughly an order of magnitude — IBKR's published Pro and Non-Pro fee columns make the split explicit, as does its rule that market data is switched off entirely below USD 500 account equity. A third cliff is regulatory: read-only with no execution keeps the product clear of broker-dealer territory, but selling investment analysis for compensation can trigger investment-adviser obligations in the relevant jurisdiction. The EDGAR-derived incentives layer is the only part of the product with no per-user data licensing cost at all, which is a strong argument for it being the commercial core as well as the differentiator.

**Rejected.** Building multi-user architecture speculatively at v1 (adds entitlements, seat management and audit infrastructure — a large fraction of what makes enterprise terminals expensive — before knowing whether a second user exists); assuming personal-use data terms scale to a product (they explicitly do not: Massive's 'Non-pros only' label and Yahoo's personal-use-only framing are contractual, not advisory).

**Cost.** Single user ~$230-330/month all-in. First commercial user adds roughly $1,750-5,000/month in redistribution rights and exchange vendor fees before a single seat is sold — a step change that must be priced into any commercial plan from the outset.

## Risks

- AGPL-3.0 contamination: taking openbb as a distributed dependency obliges disclosure of complete corresponding source for the combined work. The same trap applies to backtrader and freqtrade (GPLv3) and, more subtly, to nautilus_trader (LGPL-3.0-or-later, where static linking or derivative use crosses the line). Audit every dependency licence before the walking skeleton, not before release.
- Silent EDGAR divergence: post-acceptance corrections delete and amend filings, and daily index files are never retro-corrected — only the weekly Saturday rebuild carries PAC deletes. Without the weekly reconciliation job the insider database accumulates phantom transactions invisibly, and the failure is only discovered when a user hand-checks a specific name.
- Code-signing eligibility blocker: Azure Artifact Signing individual Public Trust certificates are restricted to developers located in the US or Canada. A solo developer elsewhere cannot sign as an individual and must incorporate an eligible legal entity first, adding weeks of lead time on the critical path before any distributable binary can exist.
- Charting-engine overrun: this is the module that reliably runs 3x over naive estimates in trading applications. Deciding to build rather than buy the render loop is the single most likely cause of the project never reaching v1.
- Attribution trap: adopting Lightweight Charts without reading the NOTICE obligation means either shipping a direct competitor's logo on every chart or rewriting the charting layer late. Decide deliberately at the end of Phase 1.
- Provider churn: polygon.io 301-redirected to massive.com and the polygon extension vanished from OpenBB's tree, both within this planning window. Any hardcoded hostname, pinned certificate or unabstracted SDK call is a future outage.
- Monetisation cliff: user number two converts the operation from subscriber to redistributor. Databento prices that transition at $1,750/month, exchange vendor agreements add reporting and audit obligations, and Massive's individual plans are contractually non-professional-only.
- Professional reclassification on the user side: if the trader registers as an adviser, manages outside capital, or is compensated for analysis, exchange fees rise by roughly an order of magnitude and several current subscriptions become non-compliant overnight.
- AI hallucination liability: a knowledge base asserting a wrong insider transaction or compensation threshold is worse than none, because the user will trade on it. Provenance binding to accession numbers, KB versioning with visible diffs, and a blocking eval gate are mandatory, not optional polish.
- Yahoo/yfinance dependency creep: trivial to prototype with, legally unfixable at commercialisation because no paid Yahoo tier exists. Remove it before the skeleton becomes a product.
- CUSIP and ISIN licensing exposure: 13F holdings tables are keyed on CUSIP, so an ownership module inherently accumulates a CUSIP-keyed dataset that CUSIP Global Services has historically asserted rights over. Key internally on FIGI and treat CUSIP as a transient join key. Confidence on the specific licensing posture is low — obtain legal advice before exposing any CUSIP-searchable feature.
- Cross-vendor adjustment drift: EODHD, Massive and Databento will each return a different 'close' for the same symbol and date because back-adjustment conventions differ. This is convention, not a bug, but it is the top generator of 'the app is wrong' reports and must be handled by labelling sources rather than picking a silent winner.
- FX semantic mismatch: no consolidated tape, no NBBO, no authoritative volume, 24x5 sessions, rollover and quote-convention inversions. Treating FX with equity semantics produces subtly wrong highs, lows and volumes that erode trust across the whole product.
- 13F misrepresentation: 45 days stale, long-only, US-listed, no shorts, and subject to confidential-treatment requests. Presenting it as current ownership is a correctness bug that a sophisticated user will spot immediately.
- Sonnet 5 introductory pricing ($2.00/$10.00 per MTok) expires 2026-08-31, one week from today, reverting to $3.00/$15.00. Any KB cost model built on the introductory rate is already stale.
- Support burden underestimation: at one user this is zero, but each additional user in year one realistically costs 2-4 hours per month of support in a data-heavy product where every vendor discrepancy generates a ticket.
- Multi-monitor topology changes: undocking a laptop or disconnecting a display commonly restores windows off-screen in naive implementations, stranding the user with an unusable application and no obvious recovery path.
- Enterprise pricing opacity: Bloomberg, LSEG, FactSet, S&P Capital IQ Pro, Morningstar Direct, Tegus, Daloopa, 2iQ and Smart Insider publish nothing. Any competitive positioning that quotes specific numbers for these is fabricated — argue from structure (annual, per-seat, sales-gated) instead.

## Open questions

- Azure Artifact Signing Basic and Premium base monthly prices and per-signature overage rates are no longer publicly listed (every money cell renders as '$-' as of 2026-08-24). A sales quote is required before this line can be budgeted; historically-cited figures are unverifiable and should not be used.
- SciChart, LightningChart and comparable commercial .NET chart control pricing could not be verified — scichart.com/pricing returned HTTP 403. Confirm per-developer per-year cost, whether royalty-free redistribution is included, and whether source-code tiers are needed before committing to the buy decision.
- Fintel and Quiver Quantitative pricing pages are fully JS-rendered and returned 403 / empty content to both fetchers. Their tier structures matter because they are the closest direct competitors on the incentives pillar.
- WhaleWisdom subscription tiers and prices could not be retrieved (404 on two candidate URLs). Relevant as the 13F-specific price anchor.
- Massive/Polygon Currencies (FX) tier pricing did not render server-side — the tab exists but prices were not in the returned content. Needed to complete the FX cost model.
- TradingView Advanced Charts licensing terms, attribution obligations and the approval-process lead time are not published; only the marketing claim 'Free and feature-rich' is public. Requires direct contact with TradingView to evaluate.
- Whether Sentry supports native Windows minidump crash reporting at the Team tier ($26/mo) was not confirmed on its pricing page. If not, a Crashpad plus self-hosted symbol server path adds roughly 1-2 engineer-weeks.
- Exact CTA/UTP Plan professional and non-professional per-user monthly rates could not be retrieved (utpplan.com fee page rendered navigation only; ctaplan.com returned 404). These are the precise numbers behind the redistribution cliff and must be obtained before any multi-user costing.
- Whether CUSIP Global Services actively enforces licensing against products that store CUSIPs extracted from public 13F filings — the legal posture is genuinely unclear and warrants counsel before building any CUSIP-searchable feature.
- Coverage and pricing for non-US insider transaction data (UK MAR Article 19 PDMR notifications, EU MAR, Japan, Canada). Both 2iQ and Smart Insider publish nothing, and this directly determines whether 'global equities' plus 'insiders' is deliverable at a solo-trader price point or must be scoped to US-only at v1.
- Whether the target user is or will become a Professional subscriber under exchange definitions — this single classification changes the data budget by roughly an order of magnitude and should be settled before any data contract is signed.
- Investing.com Pro, Seeking Alpha Premium, Stock Rover, Simply Wall St, Wisesheets, YCharts and Benzinga Pro prices could not be verified — all render pricing client-side. They matter only as secondary anchors, so low priority.
- Whether Massive/Polygon 'Non-pros only' plans permit a single professional-classified user to upgrade in place, or require migration to an entirely separate business contract with different terms and minimums.
- Whether the user requires true real-time data at all, or whether 15-minute delayed suffices for their decision horizon. This is the highest-leverage open question in the entire plan: it changes the data budget by roughly $120/month, and changes the redistribution economics by an order of magnitude if the product ever ships to a second user.
