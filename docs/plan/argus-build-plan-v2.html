JOB

What this is actually for

stated the way I'd state it to a desk

Most trading software answers the wrong question. It tells you what the price did, in more colours and on more timeframes than anyone needs. Price is the most crowded data in the industry and the least differentiated — everybody has it, it's free, and it's already in everybody else's model.

The question worth asking is different: who knows more than me, and what are they doing about it? That question has a good answer, and it's a boring one. The people who know more are frequently compelled to tell you. Not out of generosity — because regulators force them.

The through-line: compelled disclosure

A CFO buying her own stock has to file within two business days. A fund crossing 5% has to file within five. A miner hedging next year's production shows up in a positioning report every Friday. A central bank buying gold reports it to the IMF. A director pledging shares to a bank discloses it in the proxy. A company must publish which metric its CEO is paid to hit.

None of these people want to tell you. All of them have to. That obligation trail is the one dataset in finance where the informed participant is legally required to show their hand, and it is almost entirely free at origin. Everything in this plan is built to collect it, normalise it, and put it next to the price.

The second thing, which matters more than people admit

Compelled disclosure tells you what informed people chose to do. The higher-quality signal is what people are forced to do. Forced flow doesn't care about your thesis and doesn't change its mind:

What I'm not building, and why

I've watched a lot of terminals get built. They fail the same way: they add surface until nothing is trustworthy, and the honest answer to "where did this number come from?" becomes "somewhere".

  • No execution, no broker connection. You trade in MT5. This informs, it doesn't act. That's a smaller regulatory object, a smaller security object, and a smaller failure mode.
  • No prediction engine. No "AI price target". If I can't show you the evidence and the thing that would falsify it, I'd rather show you nothing.
  • No second user, ever. The moment someone else sees the screen, this stops being a subscriber and becomes a distributor. Same feed, three orders of magnitude in cost. It's an architectural invariant with a test, not a note in a README.
  • No plugins, no third-party extensions. The AI reads attacker-controlled text — filings and news. Every extension point is a hole in the only boundary keeping that safe.
  • No fake numbers to look complete. Retail FX "volume" is a tick count. If I don't have volume, the panel says so.

The thing that actually loses money

Not direction. In twenty years of watching people blow up, almost none of it was "they were wrong about the trade". It was size, stops inside the noise, holding through an event they knew was coming, and trading bigger after a loss.

So the terminal computes what most tools leave to your head: position size from your actual stop distance, whether that stop sits inside normal volatility, what the spread costs as a percentage of the risk you're taking, and what your own record says about this setup at this time of day. That's not a discipline lecture — it's arithmetic you'd otherwise do badly under pressure.

SCRN

The screens

nine · live HTML, not pictures

I think in screens, so this is where the plan gets concrete. These are rendered HTML — the density, type, colour and column structure below are the specification. Every figure is stamped SAMPLE DATA: real tickers, illustrative everything else. Nothing here records an actual transaction by an actual person.

What the screens are arguing

  • Density is the feature. 20–24px rows, hairline rules, tabular figures, no cards, no shadows. Whitespace in a blotter is paid for in scroll events.
  • Every number carries its own vintage. A 13F row is 45 days stale by law, short interest is bi-monthly, a Form 4 is near-live. Nothing is blended into a single "as of".
  • Fact and inference are different surfaces, not different tones. Facts come from deterministic pipelines with no model in the path. Any numeric AI claim without a citation is struck before render.
  • Failure is a designed screen. A dead provider greys its panel and stamps the age of the last good value. It never renders stale data as live, and never silently fails over.
  • Both asset classes get the same rigour. Screen 01 puts equities, FX and metals side by side, each carrying the lens that applies to it.

What I am arguing with these

  • Density is not a failure of design. 20–24px rows, hairlines, tabular figures, no cards, no drop shadows. Whitespace in a blotter is paid for in scroll events, and a trader scanning forty rows does not want them to breathe.
  • Every number carries its own age. A 13F row is 45 days stale by law; a Form 4 is near-live. They never share an "as of". This is the single decision that most separates a terminal you trust from one that is merely fast.
  • Fact and inference are different surfaces, marked at glyph level — not by background tint, which people stop seeing within a week and which survives neither screenshot nor clipboard.
  • Failure is a designed screen. A dead feed greys its panel and stamps the age of the last good value. It never renders stale as live, and never silently swaps to another source.
  • Both asset classes carry full weight. Screen 01 is equities, FX and metals side by side, each with the lens that applies to it.

Who is buying it with their own money?

The flagship, and the reason the rest exists. Everything here turns on one distinction: most Form 4 activity is payroll, not opinion. Measured on a recent quarter, 67% of rows are non-discretionary — awards, tax withholding, option mechanics — and open-market purchases are just 5.5%, about 22,700 a year across the whole market. A product that shows you "insider activity" without decoding the code is showing you a company's HR department.

The second distinction is who. A director who buys every January carries no information; the filer who has never bought in this month in three years and just did, carries most of it. That split is the single most valuable transform in this plan.

Who owns it, and who is short it?

Ownership data is a minefield of vintages. A 13F is 45 days late by law and can describe a position 135 days old; it shows longs only, no shorts, no derivatives. Shown honestly it is useful context. Shown as though it were current it is a liability.

The short side has a quiet scandal in it: the US number is bi-monthly and aggregate, while the FCA and EU registers name the actual holder and update daily. Free, better, and almost nobody outside institutions uses it.

Is the company running out of money?

In the small-cap universe where insider clusters actually appear, issuance is the dominant loss mechanism — not being wrong about the business. A company with an open shelf, a cash runway under eight quarters and language quietly dropped from its risk factors will sell stock into any strength you create. This module exists to make you check that before you get excited about a cluster buy.

The valuation work is deliberately Buffett-school: owner earnings with maintenance capex stated rather than buried, reverse DCF so the market's assumption is explicit, and a capital-allocation record over a decade. Multiples are a comparison tool, not an answer.

Who is positioned, and how crowded?

Cohort positioning is the closest thing FX and metals have to insider filings, and the mapping is closer than it looks: a miner selling forward production is disposing of an asset they produce with better knowledge of its economics than anyone else — structurally the same act as a CFO selling stock.

The same caveat applies too. Much producer hedging is project-finance covenant, not a price view, exactly as most Form 4 selling is tax withholding. One decoder, two asset classes: separate the mechanical from the discretionary and only score the discretionary.

And the honest framing: positioning is fuel, not a trigger. Crowded gets more crowded.

BRAIN

The knowledge layer

what "you personally update it" actually requires

Every product is bolting a chatbot onto its documents and calling it AI. That is worth very little: it retrieves what you already had, in a worse format, with a confidence it has not earned.

What is actually useful on a desk is the thing a good analyst builds over years and then takes with them when they leave — accumulated judgement. Which metric matters for a miner and why it differs for a bank. What the last three regime breaks looked like. Which insider patterns have historically meant something and which are payroll. What is typically priced into an NFP print. That is what separates ten years on a desk from a terminal licence.

So this is not a chatbot. It is a maintained body of claims that I write, version, sign and keep current, which the terminal loads and reasons from. It needs a schema, an authoring loop, a distribution channel, a trust model and an approval gate — anything less is a text file that goes stale without telling you.

The object: a knowledge pack

A pack is a versioned set of claims. Each claim has a stable identity, a body, a confidence score, a decay date and sources. Stable identity is the whole trick: because a claim persists across versions, the terminal can compute a claim-level diff and show you precisely what changed and why — rather than handing you a new opaque blob and asking you to trust it.

FieldPurpose
idStable across versions, e.g. gold.realyield.decoupled. Never reused for a different assertion.
bodyThe claim itself. One assertion, not a paragraph of hedging.
why_it_mattersWhat it changes about a decision. A claim that changes nothing does not belong in the pack.
confidence0–1. Below 0.6 renders with a visible warning.
decaysThe date after which it must be re-verified. Expired claims render struck-through with a warning, and never silently.
sourcesWhere it came from. A claim with no source cannot be signed.
supersedesOptional — the claim id this replaces, so the lineage is auditable.

The authoring loop — how I actually update it

StageWhat happensControl
1 · ScheduleA scheduled job runs off-peak against a private repo. Pack classes are due on their own cadence, so not everything regenerates nightly.cron
2 · ResearchEach due pack is researched against its declared sources, with the prior version supplied so the model is revising rather than rewriting.web + local corpus
3 · AuthorClaims written with ids preserved where the assertion persists, superseded where it changed, retired where it no longer holds.schema-validated
4 · Self-checkEvery claim must carry a source. Numeric claims are checked against the cited source. Unsourced claims are dropped, not softened.automated
5 · GateA frozen golden question set (200+ typed questions with known answers) runs against the candidate. Regressions block publication.CI gate
6 · Adversarial gateA separate suite from the correctness set: regenerated poisoned filings, hidden-instruction fixtures, injection canaries. Eval sets catch regressions; they do not catch attacks.CI gate
7 · SignEd25519 over each pack and the index. The key lives in CI, never in the binary, and never in the repo the authoring agent can write to.key custody
8 · DistributeThe terminal polls a small signed index.json with ETag and downloads only changed packs.bandwidth-cheap
9 · VerifySignature checked before load. Failure keeps the previous version and raises a visible alert — it fails closed.hard fail
10 · ReviewPer pack class: auto, review-before-live, or pinned. The claim diff is what you review, not the file.your call
11 · RollbackPrevious versions retained on disk; revert is one click and takes effect without a restart.one click

Two constraints that shape this, honestly stated

  • A scheduled agent on a consumer plan is capped at single-digit-to-low-tens of runs per day. A "one job per pack class" design across dozens of packs is therefore impossible. The pipeline is one scheduled job that batches many packs per run — which is also where batch pricing lives, so it is cheaper as well as feasible.
  • This lives beside the product, not inside the binary. The authoring pipeline runs on my side against a private repo; the terminal only ever consumes signed output. That is a better design anyway — the signing key never ships to you — but it does mean the update loop is a service you rely on, not a feature you own outright. If that pipeline stops, the terminal keeps working with the last verified packs and tells you they are ageing.

Retrieval over your own corpus

  • Structure-aware chunking of filings by Item, so a citation resolves to a section rather than an arbitrary window.
  • XBRL facts in preference to prose wherever a number exists in both. Never ask a model to read a figure it could look up.
  • Hybrid retrieval — BM25 plus dense — with reranking, because filing language is formulaic and lexical match matters.
  • Corpus capped to the watchlist (~200k chunks). Embedding everything is ~4.7M chunks and turns a sub-100ms lookup into a multi-second scan.
  • Freshness weighting, so the current 10-K outranks the 2019 one unless you asked historically.
  • Citation granularity to (accession, byte offset, length, hash), with the immutable original retained — which turns a citation chip from a presentation feature into a tamper-evident chain.

One interface per capability, never per vendor — vendors get replaced, capabilities don't. A router picks the cheapest capable source per request. Being single-user deletes most of a normal threat model: no multi-tenancy, no account takeover, no authentication surface, no session management. What's left is smaller but sharper, and one mitigation that almost every desktop finance app ships is largely theatre against the adversary you actually have. DPAPI is not protecting you, and I'd rather you knew

CryptProtectData binds a secret to your user account — which means any process running as you can call CryptUnprotectData and get the plaintext. That is precisely, literally, what the current infostealer families do; it is their main loop. Chrome's App-Bound Encryption tried to fix this exact class of problem with an elevation-service identity check and was bypassed in about 45 days, then broken structurally.

What genuinely raises the bar: CNG with VBS / Credential-Guard-class key isolation, or the TPM platform provider, plus a user passphrase (Argon2id) so the ciphertext isn't silently machine-recoverable. SQLCipher, EFS and BitLocker protect you against a stolen laptop — a real threat, but not this one.

And now the part I'd insist on saying, because it's narrower than it looks. VBS isolation stops the key being stolen. It does not stop it being used. A CNG persisted key is protected by a container ACL naming the creating user, so malware running as you can simply ask the key to decrypt and read plaintext back out of the oracle. The real gain is defeating offline and passive file theft, and forcing the attacker to stay resident on the machine — which only helps if something is looking for residency. So: plan the detection, or don't claim the benefit. Half of security engineering is refusing to bank a control you haven't finished building.

The exploit chain adversarial review found, which I'd not have caught unprompted

The natural-language screener compiles model output into SQL and runs it against DuckDB. Chain that to untrusted content and you have injected filing text → model-authored query → database engine. DuckDB by default can INSTALL and LOAD extensions, read http:// URLs, and COPY TO a UNC path. That's arbitrary read and exfiltration, launched from a hostile footnote, through a feature that looks like a safety property because "the model never produces the rows, it only produces the query".

The fix is cheap and has to be in from day one, because retrofitting it means auditing every query path: generated SQL runs on a separate read-only connection with extension loading, external filesystem and network access all disabled, against an allow-list of queryable views rather than raw tables, with a statement timeout and a parsed-AST check that rejects anything which isn't a single SELECT. Better still, compile to a restricted query AST rather than to SQL text at all — then the dangerous constructs have no way to be expressed.

Things I'd refuse to ship

  • DPAPI alone for secrets, presented as protection. It isn't, against the realistic adversary, and describing it as protection is worse than having nothing because it stops you building the real control.
  • A "not financial advice" disclaimer standing in for a design control. Disclaimers constrain nobody's behaviour. Architecture does.
  • Trusting the model to obey a prompt-level rule that matters. If it matters, it's enforced in code. A prompt is a preference, not a boundary.
  • Blanket telemetry with an opt-out. Local-only by default, explicit opt-in to send.
  • Claiming zero data retention that the account tier does not actually provide.
  • Storing the pack signing key anywhere an automated agent can reach it. The moment the thing that authors claims can also sign them, the signature stops meaning anything.

The regulatory perimeter, which is simpler than people fear and trickier than they assume

So long as it's your money and your eyes only, the advice perimeter doesn't bite. You cannot advise yourself, so the Advisers Act, FCA Article 53, the financial promotions regime, MiFID II advice and MAR Art.20 recommendation-disclosure are all inapplicable. That has a design consequence I'd make the most of: the AI can be as opinionated and directive as you want it to be. This plan assumes it should be. Hedged, lawyered output is worse than useless on a desk.

But the trigger is a second person's money or eyes — not a second install. A family member's account, an informal partnership, a fund seeded later, or simply forwarding the AI's output to a friend each cross that line while the software remains a single seat. An earlier draft of this document got that wrong, which is exactly the sort of error that feels safe until it isn't.

Two exposures the single-user framing does not clear

  • Data protection on third parties. The insider archive and person graph are, in plain terms, a profiling database of named natural persons — directors, officers, PDMRs — held for years and enriched with behavioural scoring. UK data protection has a personal-household exemption, but it is read narrowly, and a systematic profiling database built for financial gain sits near its edge. Concretely: keep a register of every table holding identified persons, set a retention policy, and don't extend the graph to inferred personal attributes. There's also a sui generis database right question over the archive itself.
  • EU AI Act: out of scope, but only because you're UK-resident. Its transparency duties attach to a provider, and "putting into service" expressly includes supply for one's own use — so an EU-resident building this for himself could be caught at a single user. Revisit if you relocate.

If a second user ever appears, everything changes at once: you become a redistributor, exchange fees multiply per subscriber, vendor agreements and audit clauses attach, the advice perimeter reappears, and you need an EULA, authentication and a support burden. That's why no-distribution is an architectural invariant with a test rather than a line in a README, and why the MAR wrapper ships dormant rather than absent — dormant is a day's work to enable, absent is a quarter.

Financial UI is where designers who've never used the thing do the most damage. The instinct is to make it calmer, lighter, more spacious — and every one of those moves costs the user information per glance. Two bindings that look obvious and are wrong

Ctrl+Alt must not be a modifier family. AltGr emits Ctrl+Alt on international layouts, and Microsoft's own guidance is to avoid it. On your UK layout, AltGr+4 (the € key) would fire a link-channel change — a bug that presents as "the app randomly changes my symbols" and takes a fortnight to attribute. Ctrl+Space is the IME toggle on CJK layouts, so the most obvious binding for a command line is the one that breaks it. Both are avoided above, and a keyboard layout guard detects the active layout at first run and on change, warning about collisions. Roughly twenty lines of code to prevent a silent degradation that produces no error message.

Red/green fails for roughly 1 in 12 men. So the default directional palette is Okabe-Ito blue and vermilion, with classic red/green available as an opt-in rather than the other way round. Uptick/downtick flashing is capped at three per second and degrades to a steady tint above that rate.

Where density and WCAG genuinely conflict — and I won't paper over it

Most terminals claim both. They don't fully co-exist. WCAG 2.2's target-size criterion effectively requires 24px between the centres of adjacent undersized targets. A 22px compact row and an 18px dense row both fail it; only the 28px comfortable row passes. Two further traps worth naming: as-of provenance stamps at 10–12px count as normal text under the guidelines rather than large text, so they need the full 4.5:1 — which makes the trust layer the thing most likely to fail the audit — and channel hues must not be reused for direction or status.

So the honest resolution is: Comfortable is the conformant default, and Compact and Dense are an informed opt-out that the settings screen labels as exactly that. Claiming blanket AA while shipping 20px rows is an accessibility statement that is technically a lie, and I'd rather ship a true narrow claim than a false broad one.

Accessibility commitments

  • Global Freeze (Alt+P) halts all animation and pins every visible value with its freeze timestamp. WCAG requires a pause mechanism for auto-updating content lasting over five seconds, so this is a requirement rather than a nicety — and it happens to be exactly what you want at 09:30 anyway. The copy must not promise buffered replay: dropped updates are dropped, and saying otherwise promises data the system threw away.
  • Screen readers get UIA notification events with most-recent processing. Never a live region on the tape, which produces continuous unusable speech.
  • 200% font scaling without layout collapse; reduced-motion honoured throughout.
  • Keyboard-only operation audited as a release gate, not asserted in a document.
  • Colour-blind modes: Okabe-Ito default, plus cyan/magenta and classic red/green, with a display calibration card at onboarding that renders all palettes, link chips at true pixel size, a 4.5:1 reference pair and a flash sample — because WPF provides no colour-managed sRGB pipeline, and wide-gamut or HDR displays demonstrably shift these.

Four questions only you can answer

None of these block the plan; each one changes its size, so I'd want them settled before Phase 2 rather than during it.

  1. Which equity universe? US-only is materially smaller than global, and the non-US insider regimes are the single most expensive optional block in the document.
  2. How much intraday equity depth do you actually need? Level 2 and microstructure carry a real data cost and a large build. The incentives pillar needs neither.
  3. Does the pack approval gate default to auto or review? Auto is convenient and means your knowledge base changes under you without asking. Review is safer and adds a daily task. I'd start on review and relax it once you trust the diffs.
  4. What's your appetite for phase 7 at all? Everything through phase 6 is a coherent, finished product. Phase 7 roughly doubles the build for depth you may never need.
