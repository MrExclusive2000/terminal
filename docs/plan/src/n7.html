COST

Money and time

the arithmetic, unrounded

Two numbers decide whether this is a project or a daydream: what it costs to run, and how long it takes to build. The first is genuinely low, and that's the whole commercial argument. The second is large, and I'd rather put it in front of you at full size than discover it together in year two.

Running cost

The reason this design is affordable is that the differentiating data is free at origin. EDGAR, the CFTC, FRED, the FCA and EU short registers, the IMF, the World Gold Council and Swiss customs all publish for nothing. The expensive feeds in a Bloomberg bill are price and news — the two things you already have from your broker and the open web.

The number I'd be sacked for hiding

96 engineer-weeks to v1 is the irreducible figure — the work assuming nothing goes wrong, which it will. Apply an honest solo multiplier of 1.6–2.5× and v1 is 154–240 engineer-weeks. At a realistic solo throughput of about 0.65 productive weeks per calendar week, that's four and a half to seven years. Full scope including phase 7 runs longer.

That is what "don't miss any features out" costs, stated plainly rather than buried in an appendix. It is not an argument against the scope — it's the information you need in order to decide about the scope, and that decision is yours, not mine. Three things make this estimate self-correcting rather than a guess: an effort ledger from week one, so your real multiplier is known after ten features; a feature ceiling, because solo maintenance at 182 features consumes 20–30 weeks a year against a capacity of roughly 34; and the phase boundaries above, every one of which is a defensible place to stop and still own something.

Where estimates habitually break

These are the five that have overrun on every desk system I've watched get built. Budgeting them at 1× is how a two-year plan becomes a five-year one.

ModuleTypical overrunWhy
Symbology & corporate actions3–5×Endless edge cases and no glory whatsoever. Ticker reuse, spin-offs, delistings, double adjustment. Nobody budgets for it and everybody pays for it.
Charting3–4×The last 10% of interaction polish is half the total work, and it's the half users notice.
Real-time infrastructure2–4×Reconnect, gap-fill, backpressure, conflation. The happy path is a week; the unhappy paths are a quarter.
Entitlements2–3×Every provider models them differently and none document them fully.
Release-calendar scheduler2–3×Looks trivial. DST, holidays, delayed publications, revised vintages and half-days all land here.
CUT

What I'd cut, and what I got wrong

the part that decides whether this ships

Everything above is the full scope you asked for, and it's all in. This section is the other half of the job: telling you what I'd drop first if reality intervenes, and what earlier drafts of this plan got wrong before review caught it.

If it has to be smaller

The minimum slice that delivers something nobody sells is Phase 0 + Phase 1 — 26 irreducible weeks: a working insider and incentives engine on entirely free data. If your trading matters more than the research surface, Phase 3 alone is a smaller alternative first target at 24 weeks — FX and metals positioning, which is closer to what you actually trade day to day. Those are the two honest starting points; anything smaller is a toy.

Cut candidates, in the order I'd drop them. None of them touches the pillar:

  1. Microstructure and replay. Large build, real data cost, and the incentives work needs neither.
  2. Segment workbench. Genuinely useful, entirely deferrable.
  3. PGM and base metals. You trade gold. Silver is a cheap add later; platinum is not.
  4. The non-US insider regimes. These cost disproportionately: UK RNS is a commercial feed, and Canada and India have no bulk API — so three regimes consume most of the block's effort for a fraction of its value.
  5. Congressional trading. Popular, thin, and slow to file.
  6. The entity graph. The most impressive demo in the plan and the least load-bearing. It's also the piece that most enlarges the data-protection exposure in BREAK.

Four adversarial passes ran against earlier drafts — completeness, feasibility and cost, security and legal, and UX — returning 121 gaps and 42 corrections. These are the ones that changed a conclusion rather than adding detail. I'm listing them because a plan whose author claims to have been right throughout is a plan nobody stress-tested.

Four questions only you can answer

None of these block the plan; each one changes its size, so I'd want them settled before Phase 2 rather than during it.

  1. Which equity universe? US-only is materially smaller than global, and the non-US insider regimes are the single most expensive optional block in the document.
  2. How much intraday equity depth do you actually need? Level 2 and microstructure carry a real data cost and a large build. The incentives pillar needs neither.
  3. Does the pack approval gate default to auto or review? Auto is convenient and means your knowledge base changes under you without asking. Review is safer and adds a daily task. I'd start on review and relax it once you trust the diffs.
  4. What's your appetite for phase 7 at all? Everything through phase 6 is a coherent, finished product. Phase 7 roughly doubles the build for depth you may never need.
